\section{Error Analysis and Interpretation}\label{sec:errors}

This section analyses the five model blend on the holdout (RMSE 431.0, MAE 314.5, bias $-47.0$~SGD), with tuned LightGBM on the index target as the comparison (445.8, 323.9, $-24.5$~SGD). The case review and the comparison with Ridge use tuned LightGBM only. Residual means prediction minus observed rent.

\subsection{Why the Error Is Not Lower}\label{sec:floor}

Rows in the same cell and month have identical inputs, including the lag features, so any model gives them one prediction. The spread of rent inside such a group is error that no model can remove. Even with the true mean of every group, the RMSE would stay at 178~SGD on all training rows, 165~SGD on the holdout and 187~SGD on the 16 month window. This bound is far too low to reach, because it counts the 74\% of rows with unique inputs as predicted perfectly.

The spread inside the groups is a better guide. Rows that share inputs have a pooled standard deviation of 449~SGD on the holdout and 468~SGD on all training rows. On 4{,}207 repeated holdout rows, the mean rent of the other rows in the group scores 612~SGD against 446~SGD for tuned LightGBM and 441~SGD for the blend, so even the rent of a near identical flat is a noisy guide. If rows with unique inputs have a similar spread, an RMSE of 431~SGD is close to what these inputs allow. This estimate is not a proven floor, and it holds only for a fixed set of inputs: the repeat-rent features lowered the RMSE because they add information that G0+G1 lacks.

Public leaderboard scores of about 160 to 180 sit near this bound. We infer that those teams look up test rents in the public HDB rental table~\cite{hdb2026renting}, which records month, town, block, street, flat type and rent and covers the test period. A lookup returns the rents of a group, so it can reach the bound but not pass it. From the given inputs alone, a score of 200 would need rows with unique inputs predicted within about 107~SGD, while rents in one group have a standard deviation of about 450~SGD. Our models use no rents from this table.

\subsection{Residuals}\label{sec:residuals}

A few large errors carry much of the squared error. Absolute errors above SGD 1{,}000 occur in 3.3\% of holdout rows and give 34\% of the squared error, against 3.9\% and 38\% for tuned LightGBM (Fig.~\ref{fig:outliers}). The 585 rows with a rent below SGD 2{,}000 are 3.4\% of the rows and give 24\% of the squared error, with a bias of $+1{,}011$~SGD. Of the 20 largest errors, 19 are over-predictions, and eight have a rent below SGD 1{,}000. In two pairs of rows the recorded profile is identical while the rents are SGD 3{,}550 and 800, and SGD 2{,}880 and 700.

The data cannot explain these rows. They may be a room or one tenant's share of the rent, while area and type describe the whole flat. They may also be discounted agreements or errors in the declared amount. One SGD 650 row matches the public HDB table~\cite{hdb2026renting}, so the amount was published. HDB states that owners declare these rents and that it does not verify them, so the match does not explain the amount.

Large flats and central areas do not carry most of the error. The 5-room and executive flats are 28\% of the rows and 31\% of the squared error, while 3-room and 4-room flats give 66\%. Rows within 5~km of Raffles Place are 12\% of the rows and 16\% of the squared error. Jurong West gives the largest share of any town (9.4\%), and it also has the most holdout rows (Fig.~\ref{fig:town-errors} in Appendix~\ref{app:eda}).

The blend gains most on rows with unique inputs, where the RMSE falls from 445.8 to 427.8~SGD. On rows that share inputs it falls from 445.8 to 440.6~SGD. Lag features can move the prediction for a whole group, but they cannot separate rows inside it.

Fig.~\ref{fig:monthly} shows no growth in error across the six holdout months for the single models, and the blend stays between 421 and 445~SGD per month. Its bias reaches $-67$~SGD in the last month, against a range of $-36$ to $-53$~SGD before it. Predicted rent deciles have a bias of $-74$ to $-18$~SGD, so the blend runs slightly low on average and misses most at the two tails.

\subsection{Importance and Partial Dependence}\label{sec:interpretation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:importance} shows permutation importance for the blend. Flat type ordinal (+44~SGD) and the 24 month rent lags (+43~SGD) have the largest effects, ahead of flat type (+17~SGD), subzone (+13~SGD) and latitude (+12~SGD). The 12 month lags add +5~SGD and the 36 month lags +0.4~SGD, so the signal sits mostly at two years, and that feature set stays available through the test period. Tuned LightGBM alone puts more weight on fewer inputs: flat type ordinal +166~SGD, subzone +112~SGD and floor area +26~SGD. Related inputs share importance, and the blend spreads it further across its members. Month has the strongest numeric rank correlation with rent, but its effect here is only +0.1~SGD. The rent index carries the trend and is nearly flat over the holdout, so this result does not mean that time is unimportant.

Fig.~\ref{fig:pdp} compares tuned LightGBM with a Ridge model on the same 39 inputs and the index target (RMSE 467.1, slightly different from the baseline Ridge in Table~\ref{tab:results}). Ridge has a straight response to MRT distance. LightGBM falls 46~SGD from 0.18 to 0.40~km and 33~SGD from there to 1.15~km. When lease year moves together with age, a flat 43 years older loses 210~SGD under LightGBM and 581~SGD under Ridge. LightGBM has lower error in every MRT distance band, and its gain varies by segment: Bukit Merah 3-room flats improve from 545 to 479~SGD, while Jurong West executive flats worsen from 580 to 601~SGD. These gains fit a nonlinear response and interactions, but they do not show which of the two caused the improvement.
